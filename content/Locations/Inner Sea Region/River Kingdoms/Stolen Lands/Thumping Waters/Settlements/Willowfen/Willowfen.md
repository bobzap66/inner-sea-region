# Willowfen


### Willowfen

Barely a few months old, **Willowfen** is the newest and muddiest jewel in the growing crown of the Republic of Thumping Waters. Nestled in a low, mist-draped basin of the northern **Narlmarches**, the village seems to have sprung up almost by accident—part settlement, part experiment, and part stubborn declaration that “yes, people can live here, probably.” Though small and perpetually damp, Willowfen already hums with life, laughter, and the rhythmic squelch of boots that may never be clean again.

#### Overview

Willowfen stands about fifty miles west of Thumpington, surrounded by dense forest and soft, springy ground that refuses to decide whether it’s soil or sponge. The **wooden walls** encircling the settlement are more symbolic than defensive, built from green pine logs that still smell of sap. Within them, a few dozen **houses** perch on short stilts to keep the floors above the morning fog. Trails of packed dirt—called “streets” by the optimistic—wind between them, lined with planks for when the ground grows treacherous after rain.

At the town’s modest center stands the **general store**, doubling as supply depot, notice board, and rumor exchange. Opposite it sits *The Silver Reed Inn*, a cheerful two-story lodge whose porch remains the driest spot in the settlement by virtue of relentless sweeping and sheer willpower. The newly raised **town hall** serves as a meeting place, supply shed, and refuge during the occasional flood of enthusiasm that the locals insist on calling “weather.”

#### History

Willowfen was founded only months ago by a mix of loggers, trappers, and fisherfolk eager to push deeper into the Narlmarches. The Tubthumpers authorized the charter after surveyors declared the site “mostly stable, provided you don’t jump.” Work began immediately—axes ringing through the mist, fires glowing in the fog, and tents slowly giving way to timber walls. The settlement’s name came from the narrow, ghostly willows that ring the clearing, their silver leaves whispering in the wind like rain. According to local legend, Mairi herself visited the site during its founding and declared, “It’s wet, it smells strange, and it’ll probably survive. Build it.”

Since then, Willowfen has grown at a reckless pace. Every week brings another wagonload of settlers or lumber, and the smell of freshly cut pine lingers in the air. The people have no illusions about the hardships—they speak openly about the insects, the chill, and the mud that eats footwear with alarming hunger—but none seem ready to give up. In their own words, “If the trees can root here, so can we.”

#### Government and Life

Leadership in Willowfen is as temporary as the ground is solid. The acting mayor, **Tarin Veln**, was elected mostly because he owned a hammer and knew how to use it. He oversees daily work from the **town hall**, coordinating supply shipments and trying to keep the walls upright through the combined efforts of carpenters, optimism, and rope. The **general store** is run by Mara Olegsdottir, whose stock varies wildly depending on what she can get from Olegton’s caravans—one week filled with lantern oil and saw blades, the next week nothing but turnips and soap.

Life in Willowfen follows the rhythm of the forest. Settlers rise early to fell trees, build, and clear ground for future fields. Evenings bring laughter and exhaustion at *The Silver Reed Inn*, where muddy boots and bad singing are equally welcome. The townsfolk are friendly but practical; they’ll share a meal, a drink, or a spare boardwalk plank, but they have no patience for arrogance. Everyone works here. Everyone gets dirty.

#### Culture and Role

Though young, Willowfen has already found its purpose: it’s a staging post for the republic’s expansion into the western woods and a symbol of how quickly the frontier can grow civilized when given a little effort and a lot of stubbornness. Travelers from Thumpington stop to rest and swap stories, merchants deliver supplies, and trappers bring in game from the Narlmarches’ deeper reaches. The village has even begun hosting small fairs on dry days—music, stew, and wagers on whose boots will sink first in the mud outside the gate.

At night, Willowfen glows softly under its lanterns, their reflections caught in the fog. Frogs croak, insects hum, and the smell of pine smoke drifts above the roofs. The locals call it “the hum of home.” It isn’t grand, but it’s alive—and for a settlement born only months ago, that’s triumph enough.

Daily life here follows the rhythm of the forest. Mornings begin with the sound of axes and gulls, afternoons with the splash of nets and the hum of saws, and evenings with smoke curling from hearths and laughter rising from *The Silver Reed*. The general store closes only when its owner, Mara Olegsdottir, decides the conversation has gone on long enough—which is rarely. Strangers are welcomed with curiosity but judged by how they treat the marsh. Anyone who mocks its mud or kills more fish than they can eat will find their welcome evaporating faster than summer rain.

#### Culture and Role

Willowfen serves as a waystation between Thumpington and the deeper Narlmarch settlements, a place to rest, resupply, and swap rumors before vanishing into the woods again. Its residents take pride in their resilience and their hospitality—though both are edged with dry humor. They tell newcomers, “If you can stand the mud, you can stand with us.” They mean it. Life here is damp, unpredictable, and beautiful in its own strange way. It’s the sort of place that teaches patience, rewards resourcefulness, and breeds the kind of stubborn joy that only frontier towns ever seem to find.

At night, the town glows like a lantern in the marsh, reflections of its fires flickering across still black water. The frogs sing, the reeds whisper, and somewhere in the mist, an owl calls. For travelers heading west, that sound is a benediction: the promise that they are not lost, not yet. For the people of Willowfen, it’s simply home.

## General Details

##### From the Journal of Linzi, Minister of Communication of Thumping Waters

*The Soggy Birth of Civilization*

Willowfen is what happens when optimism wins an argument with common sense. It’s the newest settlement in the Republic—so new, in fact, that the paint on the **town hall** hasn’t dried and the **inn** is still waiting for its second wall. The founding charter is barely older than the mud it’s written on, but somehow the place already has a general store, an elected mayor, and at least three different theories about what the smell is (all confident, none comforting).

The road there ends about two miles too soon, which I assume is deliberate—possibly to weed out the faint of heart. When I arrived, I was greeted by a cheerful man standing ankle-deep in what I can only describe as *ambitious moisture*. “Welcome to Willowfen!” he said. “Don’t stand still too long or the ground gets ideas.” He introduced himself as Mayor Tarin Veln, a man who runs a settlement the way most people run on caffeine and denial. He gave me a tour that consisted largely of pointing at piles of lumber and saying, “That’ll be something important by next week.”

The *Silver Reed Inn* was open, which is a relative term. There was a roof, three walls, and a bar counter that doubled as a support beam. The barkeep, **Edda Fenlock**, handed me a tankard before I could ask what was in it and declared, “Welcome, Minister! Don’t mind the slope—it’s settling.” The inn tilts slightly southeast, which means every spilled drink eventually finds the same corner, and by evening it forms a communal puddle of what I like to call *Republic Reserve*. Everyone pretends not to notice. Everyone slips in it anyway.

The people are absurdly cheerful. The **general store** sells nails by the pound and optimism by the grin. A carpenter waved to me with one hand while holding his roof up with the other. I saw children racing toy boats down a drainage ditch that might one day be the main road. The town’s motto, according to the mayor, is “We’ll figure it out,” which appears to double as both civic policy and daily prayer.

And yet—there’s something magnificent about it. At dusk, when the lanterns light through the mist and the new-cut pine glows gold, Willowfen looks less like a village and more like an idea taking root. It shouldn’t work. It probably shouldn’t exist. But it does. The settlers laugh, the walls stand (mostly), and even the mud seems proud to be part of something that hasn’t given up yet. I left with wet boots, a sore back, and the firm conviction that if Willowfen can survive its own enthusiasm, it’ll outlive us all.

*—Linzi*

Editor’s note: Linzi’s description of the “communal puddle” at the Silver Reed Inn has been corroborated by multiple visitors, though its contents remain a subject of ongoing scientific debate.

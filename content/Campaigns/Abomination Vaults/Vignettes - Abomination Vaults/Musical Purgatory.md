# Musical Purgatory


Light slides across the table like a half-remembered tune.
I feel the rhythm of it more than the meaning.

Why am I here?
The tavern smells of smoke and spilled ale and other people's stories. The noise presses against my head and I try to catch a thread—anything that tells me the shape of myself.

There was music. A string. A laugh that fit my chest once. My fingers want to find that string. They twitch as if the motion itself holds the name I’ve lost.
Pull—aim—loose. The motion sits in me like a footprint in soft earth: not memory, but a map. I trace it with my hands until the map feels like something true.

A name scrabbles at the edge of thought. Com— It might be right. It might be a lie. The syllable has weight, the wrong kind of weight, like a coin that might be counterfeit. I press it to my teeth to see if it rings. Nothing answers.

People speak. Their words blur into a single river of sound. I catch a laugh—bright, quick—and I practice the shape of a response until it feels less like pretending and more like habit. Smile. Make a face. Perform the small thing that turns the room into a stage. The stage steadies me. The stage is something to do.

I try to sing. The tune sticks halfway out of my throat and evaporates. I hum instead—quiet, without sound at first—and it grows until it becomes a pulse under my skin. Rhythm steadies the emptiness. Rhythm is a plan: one-two-three, one-two-three. Tap, tap—measure. Keep count so the day does not swallow me whole.

There is a pull toward the door. Move or stay. Stay and become furniture—part of the warm wood and the steady chatter. Move and become a problem, a story that starts elsewhere. Movement feels like choosing an instrument: pick it up and make something happen.

A child points. A drunk spills his mug. A stranger catches my eye and offers a coin or a nod—small acts that might be hooks. I gather them like crumbs. Crumbs make a path. Paths are better than blank floors.

Who was I before the quiet? I reach for fragments: a bowstring’s whisper, the smell of tar on a road, the warmth of a crowd when a joke lands. None of it snaps into place. The trauma that raised me cleaned the shelves and left only a few things on the counter: instincts, reflex, a habit of performance. The rest is fog.

So I wait. Not because I remember why. Not because I believe the reason will come back. Because waiting is something to do. Because a hum, a tap, a practiced motion might stitch a name onto the silence. Because once, somewhere, someone applauded and my chest lit up, and the idea of that light is enough to sit beneath for now.

Tap. Hum. Watch the door. Keep the rhythm. The rest will come if the world hands me the right next note.

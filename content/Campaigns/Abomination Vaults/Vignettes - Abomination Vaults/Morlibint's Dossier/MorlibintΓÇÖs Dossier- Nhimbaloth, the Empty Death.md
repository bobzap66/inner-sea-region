# Morlibint’s Dossier: Nhimbaloth, the Empty Death

![[shared-cover-32ff100f6011.png]]

> [!side]
> :   ![[morlibints-dossier-nhimbaloth-the-empty-death-image-01.png]]

*A collation of suppressed Pharasmin notes, unreliable field reports, and texts best left unconsulted.*

Nhimbaloth is not a god in any sense that offers comfort to the learned. She does not grant purpose, demand worship in any conventional structure, or even meaningfully acknowledge her followers. To categorize her alongside other divinities is a convenience of scholarship, not an accurate reflection of her nature. Among the few circles that dare speak of her plainly, she is called the Empty Death.

The title is not poetic. It is descriptive.

Nhimbaloth is most often associated with the River of Souls, not as a guide, guardian, or even a parasite in the traditional sense, but as an interruption. Where other entities that haunt that passage—daemons, night hags, and the like—extract value from souls in transit, Nhimbaloth simply removes them. She hunts along the metaphysical shorelines of that river, consuming both the predators that gather there and the souls they pursue. No distinction is made. No hierarchy is recognized. Once taken by her, a soul does not reach the Boneyard, is not judged, and cannot be reclaimed. It is not merely destroyed. It is rendered absent.

This detail alone has caused significant discomfort among Pharasmin scholars, though such discomfort is rarely recorded in official doctrine.

# Manifestation and Signs

Nhimbaloth does not possess a stable form. Most credible accounts insist she has none at all, existing instead as a presence that can only be inferred through its effects. Under rare and highly specific conditions—alignments of time, death, and perhaps intent—she may manifest briefly in a perceivable state. Witnesses describe a shifting mass of viscous blue substance, threaded with translucent tendrils and punctuated by yellow, four-pointed eyes. Each tendril divides into seven finer strands, and where these touch the world, they leave behind a ring of seven evenly spaced divots.

These markings are the most reliable indicator of her passage.

Where they appear, the consequences are consistent. Vegetation withers or becomes aggressively hostile. Animals abandon natural patterns in favor of erratic or violent behavior. Most tellingly, the dead do not pass on cleanly. Faceless undead begin to manifest, lacking identity, memory, or purpose. They do not haunt for revenge or unfinished business. They simply persist, as though something has prevented them from being properly claimed.

It is tempting to interpret this as corruption. It is more accurate to view it as interference.

# On Will-o’-Wisps and Influence

Nhimbaloth’s connection to will-o’-wisps is widely attested, though poorly understood. Many cult traditions assert that she perceives the world through them, using their flickering lights as distant eyes. While such claims would ordinarily be dismissed as theological embellishment, the consistency of wisp activity in regions touched by Nhimbaloth suggests a relationship that warrants caution. Whether they serve her, are created by her, or are merely drawn to the same conditions remains unresolved.

Certain regions of Golarion bear more pronounced signs of her influence. The Mushfens of southern Varisia are among the most frequently cited. There, will-o’-wisp–touched individuals—often described as oracles—are said to erode the will and cognition of those they encounter. Victims are not slain outright, but reduced to wandering shells, stripped of purpose and resistance. These husks persist until death, at which point their souls are conspicuously unaccounted for.

# Cult Doctrine

Nhimbaloth’s cults are rare, fragmented, and disturbingly consistent in belief. They do not promise power or salvation. Instead, they venerate despair as the truest expression of existence. To them, the terror felt at the moment of death is not something to be avoided, but something to be embraced—the breath of their goddess brushing against the soul. They encourage the creation of undead, particularly those born of confusion, fear, or violent ends, thereby increasing the number of souls that fail to pass cleanly onward.

Their practices also include a fixation on predation. Followers are encouraged to consume creatures that have themselves recently fed upon others, mirroring what they believe to be Nhimbaloth’s indiscriminate appetite. It is less ritual than imitation.

Notably, her doctrine contains no anathema. There are no forbidden acts, no moral boundaries. This absence is not permissive—it is revealing. Nhimbaloth does not reward or punish behavior in any conventional sense. There is nothing one can do that would displease her. There is only whether one contributes to the conditions in which she manifests, or becomes subject to them.

# Divine Opposition

Among the divine, Nhimbaloth is treated not merely as an enemy, but as a problem without a clear solution. Pharasma’s opposition is self-evident; Nhimbaloth’s predations undermine the entire structure of death and judgment. Yet even Pharasmin efforts to combat her influence are measured and, in many cases, deliberately obscured from the public. This suggests not only caution, but uncertainty.

Other powers take notice as well. Gozreh’s domain suffers under the unnatural hostility of tainted wetlands, and Desna’s concern for wanderers places her followers at risk in regions where Nhimbaloth’s influence lingers. Even the Eldest are said to oppose her encroachments, implying that her reach extends beyond mortal reality into the First World.

# On Origin

There remain persistent, deeply troubling accounts regarding her origin. Certain texts—rare, fragmented, and often suppressed—suggest that Nhimbaloth was not always as she is now. That she once possessed a body, and that this form was lost in a confrontation with Pharasma. If true, this loss did not diminish her. It liberated her. Unbound by physical form, she now moves along the River of Souls itself, striking where she cannot be easily perceived or opposed.

I will not attempt to verify this claim. Its implications are sufficiently dire without embellishment.

# Conclusion

It is enough to understand this: Nhimbaloth is not merely a predator of souls. She is a disruption of the expectation that death leads somewhere. She is the possibility—however remote, however rare—that there is something between the end of life and what should come after.

And that something is hungry.

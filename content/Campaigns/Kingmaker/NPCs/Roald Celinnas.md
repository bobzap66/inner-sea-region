# Roald Celinnas

![[roald-celinnas-portrait.png]]

Roald Celinnas is the proprietor of the Crow's Feather, a crowded Pitax inn that also serves as the city's informal library. He is a bookkeeper in both senses of the word: a man who maintains an establishment of uncertain profitability and who guards one of the strangest private collections in the River Kingdoms.

Curmudgeonly, private, and far more observant than he prefers to appear, Roald became an important source of intelligence during the liberation of Pitax. He has since served as an occasional Pitax correspondent and contributing archivist for the Lantern and Ledger, although he objects whenever anyone describes him as a member of its staff.

### At a Glance

**Ancestry:** Human

**Homeland:** Pitax

**Occupation:** Innkeeper, bookseller in everything but name, scholar, and occasional correspondent

**Residence:** The Crow's Feather, Pitax

**Known Associates:** Ally Grainger, Fitzroy, Linzi, and the scholarly community of Pitax

**Notable Trait:** Roald appears to know when someone is considering stealing from his collection, sometimes before the prospective thief does.

### The Keeper of the Crow's Feather

The Crow's Feather was once a sleepy and largely unremarkable inn. Its mead remains watery, its bread habitually stale, and its stews have inspired several vigorous debates over whether disappointment can properly be considered a flavor. What changed the establishment was Roald's collection of books.

Readers may consult the collection provided they order from the inn's conspicuously overpriced menu and keep every volume inside the building. Nothing is offered for sale. Roald does accept trades, but his measure of a book's worth follows a private logic that has defeated merchants, scholars, and at least one professional appraiser. A beautifully illuminated genealogy may earn a dismissive grunt, while a water-damaged field journal can secure an unexpectedly generous exchange.

The collection turned the Crow's Feather into a meeting place for scholars, artists, students, amateur historians, and people who enjoy being corrected in public. Tables remain occupied at nearly every hour. Roald complains about the noise, the crowd, the questions, the damage done to his chairs, and the general decline of serious reading. He has nevertheless expanded the shelving more than once.

### The Box Somewhere

When asked where he obtained so many books, Roald generally says that he found them “in a box somewhere.” He offers the statement with such finality that most listeners understand it as both an answer and a refusal to provide one.

The truth is likely less simple. The collection contains works gathered from many periods, countries, and disciplines, including volumes difficult to obtain anywhere outside a major university or noble archive. Some bear signs of careful inheritance, others of hurried purchase, salvage, barter, or rescue. Roald records neither provenance nor price where a patron might conveniently inspect it.

He is believed to be the great-grandson of a Brevic wizard whose family later settled in Pitax. Roald publicly shows little interest in magic and describes his own practice as dabbling unworthy of comparison with his ancestor. Anyone who has watched him assess an enchanted manuscript, detect a concealed glamour, or appear soundlessly beside a visitor reaching toward the wrong shelf may judge that modesty according to taste.

> If you intend to steal that, put it back before you crease the spine.  
> —Roald Celinnas, reportedly spoken from another room

### The War of the River Kings

During the Tubthumpers' covert effort to liberate Pitax from Castruccio Irovetti, Ally Grainger approached Roald through the one subject more reliable than patriotism, profit, or flattery: competent discussion of arcane theory. Fitzroy later joined her in further research and conversation. Together they convinced Roald that they were more serious than the ordinary conspirators who periodically mistook his inn for a suitably dramatic meeting place.

Roald allowed the Crow's Feather to become a refuge for the resistance and opened its back rooms to those working against Irovetti. More importantly, he shared intelligence gathered through his customers and correspondence. Several of Irovetti's closest allies, he warned, were believed to be creatures capable of assuming other forms. The warning proved valuable during the assault on the palace, where appearances could not safely be trusted.

Roald has never presented himself as a revolutionary. Asked after the war why he had risked his inn and collection, he reportedly answered that Irovetti had made scholarship servile, theater flattering, and secrecy tedious. This may have been his version of a declaration of principle.

### Correspondent and Archivist

Following Pitax's liberation, the Crow's Feather remained an informal center of the city's intellectual life. Roald now exchanges information with scholars in Thumping Waters and occasionally contributes research to the Lantern and Ledger. His articles concern subjects for which rumor, folklore, and incomplete records must be compared carefully—particularly the First World, old River Kingdoms history, magical disguises, and threats hidden beneath respectable appearances.

The newspaper identifies him as its *Pitax Correspondent and Contributing Archivist*. Roald insists that he is neither employed by the paper nor responsible for anything it prints after an editor has “made the sentences cheerful.” Linzi continues to send him questions. He continues to answer enough of them to make the title useful.

His recent work on the Wild Hunt drew upon accounts collected across Pitax, testimony from the Tubthumpers, and annotations supplied by Jubilost Narthropple. The resulting article reflects Roald's preferred method: begin with what witnesses agree upon, mark what remains uncertain, and become suspicious whenever a story fits together too neatly.

### Personality

Roald's manner is dry, impatient, and frequently dismissive. He has little tolerance for grandstanding, careless handling of books, or questions asked primarily so the questioner can demonstrate having heard of the subject. He respects preparation, precise language, and people willing to admit what they do not know.

Beneath the complaints lies a strong sense of custodianship. Roald believes books should be read, arguments should be tested, and useful knowledge should reach the people capable of acting upon it. He simply sees no reason any of those activities must be accompanied by comfortable chairs or good soup.

### Linzi's Notes

> Roald once returned an article with eleven corrections, three objections, and a note explaining that he had no time to write for newspapers. The corrected article was six pages longer than the draft. I have treated this as acceptance of his position.  
> —Linzi, Minister of Communication

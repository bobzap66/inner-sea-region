# Hooktongue


### The Hooktongue Orm

#### Classification

Common Name: Hooktongue Orm
Presumed Type: Colossal serpent or draconic aberration
Habitat: The deepest reaches of Lake Hooktongue
Status: Unverified but widely feared

#### Description

The Hooktongue Orm is said to be a serpent vast enough to coil around a barge and drag it whole beneath the surface. Eyewitnesses describe a glistening black body marked by faint, bioluminescent streaks that pulse when it stirs. Its tongue, forked and crimson, is reputedly barbed — the source of its name — and capable of impaling prey before the jaws even close.

When it surfaces, the air turns sour and the water quakes, as if the lake itself draws breath. Locals insist the creature’s eyes shine like lanterns in the mist, and that to meet its gaze is to lose one’s way forever in the swamp.

#### Origins and Theories

Scholars of Thumpington debate whether the Orm is a natural serpent grown monstrous, a surviving spawn of an ancient linnorm, or a remnant of the First World left adrift when the planes parted. The druids of Sootscale Valley speak of it as a guardian spirit, once bound to keep darker things from rising through the lake’s depths. Others claim it is the very darkness they were meant to guard against.

#### Encounters and Offerings

Though the Republic has never officially confirmed its existence, fishermen and trappers lose boats every year. The surviving logs of the explorer Avenel Thorn record “a shape the size of the world tree” moving beneath his raft; his journal ends mid-sentence. The people of the Slough make small sacrifices before venturing onto the water — fish, meat, sometimes a drop of blood — and swear that the lake grows quiet afterward.

#### Current Status

The Ministry of Defense maintains that no creature of such size could survive in a lake that shallow. Nonetheless, the Republic has marked the Hooktongue region as hazardous, and vessels traveling the western channels of the Slough are required to carry a silver bell — a tradition said to drive the Orm away, or at least announce one’s presence politely.

## General Details

#### Local Legend: The Song That Woke the Water

*As told by Edda Marsh-Reed, ferryman of Thumpington’s east dock*

They say the lake wasn’t always gray. Once it was a bright river, straight and shining, so clear you could count the stars sleeping in it. Then came a woman from the north, a wanderer of light and laughter, who sang a song to steal those stars away.

Something deep beneath the water heard her. A serpent, old as roots, rose to beg her to stop. But she mocked its barbed tongue and called it “Hooktongue,” swearing she’d take every light it guarded. So the serpent struck. It bit the song in half, swallowed her whole, and sank again.

The stars it saved burned out inside it, turning the river to ash and shadow. That’s why the swamp is gray and the lake never still. On windless nights, if you listen close, you can hear her voice from below, still singing the last note that’ll wake the Hooktongue Orm once more.

#### Naturalist’s Commentary

The tale of The Song That Woke the Water persists throughout the Hooktongue Slough, and though dismissed as superstition by most citizens of the Republic, it provides curious insight into the region’s collective memory. Some scholars believe the “woman of light” represents an ancient fey or planar traveler — perhaps even a First World envoy whose interference shaped the lake’s creation.

The description of the Orm devouring her song may be a distorted retelling of an event where a primordial serpent or linnorm fought and defeated such an intruder, leaving behind the swamp’s current geography. Others suggest the story’s imagery of “swallowed stars” and “ash-gray waters” preserves a dim cultural echo of the Bloom of Monsters, when strange lights and songs were seen along the lakeshore before the first victims flowered into beasts.

Regardless of its truth, the legend of the Hooktongue Orm continues to serve an important role in local life: a warning against hubris, a reminder of unseen powers beneath the surface, and an excuse to leave a few coins or drops of blood at the water’s edge before setting sail.
